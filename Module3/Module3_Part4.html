DOCUMENT 1: 3.7 – SHAPE AND TEXTURE ANALYSIS

PART A – SHAPE ANALYSIS

1. Introduction to Shape and Texture Analysis

First, understand what happens when a computer receives an image.

Suppose we give a computer an image of an apple.

The computer performs these operations:

  1. Takes the original image.

  2. Segments the image to separate the apple from the background.

  3. Extracts features from the apple.

  4. Analyses its shape and texture.

  5. Uses these features to identify the object.

There are two important types of information:

Shape

Shape describes the geometrical structure or form of an object.

Examples:

  • Circle

  • Rectangle

  • Triangle

  • Leaf

  • Vehicle

  • Tumor

Texture

Texture describes the surface appearance or pattern of an object.

Examples:

  • Smooth

  • Rough

  • Grainy

  • Regular

  • Random

  • Repetitive

Easy difference: Shape tells us what the object looks like from its outline. Texture tells us how its surface looks.

2. Why Shape and Texture Analysis?

Imagine two objects:

  • Object A is a circle.

  • Object B is a square.

Both might have the same colour, but their shapes are different.

Now imagine two surfaces:

  • Surface A is smooth.

  • Surface B is rough.

Both might have the same average brightness, but their textures are different.

Therefore:

  • Shape helps distinguish objects based on geometry.

  • Texture helps distinguish objects based on surface patterns.

Both are useful because colour or pixel intensity alone may not be sufficient.

3. Shape Analysis

Definition: Shape analysis is the process of extracting and analysing the geometric properties of an object or region in an image.

Important shape features:

  1. Area

  2. Perimeter

  3. Centroid

  4. Bounding box

  5. Aspect ratio

  6. Circularity

  7. Compactness

  8. Orientation

  9. Eccentricity

  10. Moments

We'll understand each one separately.

4. Input to Shape Analysis

Shape analysis generally works on a segmented object or binary image.

What is a binary image?

A binary image contains only two values:

  • 0 = Background

  • 1 = Object

For example:

Plain text0000000000 0001111000 0011111100 0011111100 0001111000 0000000000

Here:

  • 0 represents the background.

  • 1 represents the object.

The computer uses the pixels having value 1 to calculate the shape features.

5. Example of Binary Object

Consider the following binary image:

Plain text0000000000 0001111000 0011111100 0011111100 0001111000 0000000000

The white/foreground pixels represent the object.

From these pixels, the computer can calculate:

  • Area

  • Perimeter

  • Centroid

  • Bounding box

  • Shape descriptors

Remember: Binary images make shape measurement easier because the object is separated from the background.

6. Area

Definition: Area is the total number of pixels belonging to an object.

Formula:

$$ A=\sum_{x,y} I(x,y) $$

For a binary image, object pixels have value 1.

Therefore, simply count the number of 1s.

Example:

If an object contains 20 foreground pixels:

$$ A=20\text{ pixels} $$

So its area is 20 pixels.

Applications:

  • Measuring the size of a tumor.

  • Measuring the size of a fruit.

  • Detecting defects in manufacturing.

7. Perimeter

Definition: Perimeter is the total length of the boundary surrounding an object.

Imagine drawing a line around an apple. The length of that boundary is its perimeter.

livephysics.com

In digital images, perimeter is estimated using boundary pixels or contours.

Example:

A square having sides of 5 units:

$$ P=4\times5=20 $$

Its perimeter is 20 units.

8. Area vs Perimeter

These two concepts are different.

AreaPerimeter
Measures the inside regionMeasures the outer boundary
Counts object pixelsMeasures boundary length
Represents object sizeRepresents boundary length

Important point:

Two objects can have similar areas but different perimeters.

An irregular object generally has a larger perimeter compared with a compact object of similar area.

9. Centroid

Definition: Centroid is the geometrical centre of an object.

In simple words, imagine placing your finger under an object at its balancing point. That point is approximately its centroid.

For a binary object:

$$ \bar{x}=\frac{\sum xI(x,y)}{\sum I(x,y)} $$
$$ \bar{y}=\frac{\sum yI(x,y)}{\sum I(x,y)} $$

Where:

  • $\bar{x}$ = X-coordinate of centroid.

  • $\bar{y}$ = Y-coordinate of centroid.

  • $I(x,y)$ = pixel value.

Example:

Suppose three object pixels are located at:

$$ (1,1),(3,1),(2,4) $$

Then:

$$ \bar{x}=\frac{1+3+2}{3}=2 $$
$$ \bar{y}=\frac{1+1+4}{3}=2 $$

Therefore:

$$ Centroid=(2,2) $$

Uses:

  • Finding object position.

  • Object alignment.

  • Tracking objects.

10. Bounding Box

Definition: A bounding box is the smallest rectangular box that completely encloses an object.

nature.com

It is represented using four coordinates:

  • $x_{\min}$: Leftmost coordinate.

  • $y_{\min}$: Topmost coordinate.

  • $x_{\max}$: Rightmost coordinate.

  • $y_{\max}$: Bottommost coordinate.

Width:

$$ Width=x_{\max}-x_{\min} $$

Height:

$$ Height=y_{\max}-y_{\min} $$

Example:

Given:

$$ x_{\min}=10,\quad x_{\max}=50 $$
$$ y_{\min}=20,\quad y_{\max}=80 $$

Width:

$$ 50-10=40 $$

Height:

$$ 80-20=60 $$

Therefore, bounding box dimensions are 40 × 60.

11. Aspect Ratio

Definition: Aspect ratio describes the relationship between the width and height of an object.

Formula:

$$ Aspect\ Ratio=\frac{Width}{Height} $$

Example:

Width = 40

Height = 20

$$ AR=\frac{40}{20}=2 $$

This means the object is twice as wide as it is tall.

Important:

  • AR = 1 means width and height are equal.

  • AR greater than 1 means wider than tall.

  • AR less than 1 means taller than wide.

12. Why Aspect Ratio Is Useful?

Consider two objects.

Object A is wide and rectangular.

Object B is narrow and vertical.

Their aspect ratios will be different.

Aspect ratio helps distinguish:

  • Vehicles.

  • Characters.

  • Industrial components.

  • Different object categories.

For example, a horizontally positioned car usually has a higher width-to-height ratio than a standing traffic cone.

13. Circularity

Definition: Circularity measures how closely an object resembles a perfect circle.

Formula:

$$ Circularity=\frac{4\pi A}{P^2} $$

Where:

  • A = Area.

  • P = Perimeter.

  • $\pi=3.14$.

For a perfect circle:

$$ Circularity=1 $$

For irregular objects, circularity is generally lower.

Example:

Area = 100

Perimeter = 40

$$ C=\frac{4\times3.14\times100}{40^2} $$
$$ C=\frac{1256}{1600} $$
$$ \boxed{C=0.785} $$

The value is below 1, meaning the object is not a perfect circle.

14. Understanding Circularity

read-en.com

  • Perfect circle → High circularity.

  • Irregular shape → Low circularity.

Remember: The closer circularity is to 1, the more circular the object is.

15. Compactness

Definition: Compactness describes how efficiently an object occupies its area relative to its boundary.

One common formula is:

$$ Compactness=\frac{P^2}{A} $$

Another related normalized measure is:

$$ \frac{4\pi A}{P^2} $$

This second formula is commonly called circularity or shape factor.

Important exam point: Different textbooks may use different definitions of compactness. Always mention the formula you are using.

16. Eccentricity

Definition: Eccentricity describes how elongated an object is.

It is calculated using the major and minor axes of an ellipse fitted to the object.

Formula:

$$ e=\sqrt{1-\frac{b^2}{a^2}} $$

Where:

  • $a$ = Semi-major axis (longer axis).

  • $b$ = Semi-minor axis (shorter axis).

Example 1: Circle

If:

$$ a=5,\quad b=5 $$
$$ e=\sqrt{1-\frac{25}{25}}=0 $$

Therefore, a circle has eccentricity 0.

Example 2: Elongated object

If:

$$ a=5,\quad b=3 $$
$$ e=\sqrt{1-\frac{9}{25}} $$
$$ e=\sqrt{0.64}=0.8 $$

Therefore, eccentricity is 0.8.

Remember:

  • Circle → Eccentricity 0.

  • More elongated object → Eccentricity closer to 1.

17. Orientation

Definition: Orientation describes the direction in which an object is positioned.

Examples:

  • Horizontal object.

  • Vertical object.

  • Diagonal object.

tandfonline.com

Orientation can be measured relative to the horizontal axis.

Applications:

  • Character recognition.

  • Vehicle analysis.

  • Object tracking.

19. Shape Moments

Definition: Moments are mathematical values that describe the spatial distribution of pixels in an image.

In simple words, moments help the computer understand how pixels are distributed inside an object.

General image moment:

$$ M_{pq}=\sum_x\sum_y x^p y^q I(x,y) $$

Where:

  • p and q = Moment orders.

  • x and y = Pixel coordinates.

  • I(x,y) = Image intensity.

Moments help describe:

  • Size.

  • Location.

  • Orientation.

  • Shape.

  • Pixel distribution.

20. Zeroth Moment

The zeroth-order moment is:

$$ M_{00}=\sum_x\sum_y I(x,y) $$

For a binary image:

$$ M_{00}=\text{Total number of object pixels} $$

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{M_{00}=Area} $$

Example:

If an object contains 50 foreground pixels:

$$ M_{00}=50 $$

21. First-Order Moments

First-order moments are:

$$ M_{10}=\sum_x\sum_y xI(x,y) $$
$$ M_{01}=\sum_x\sum_y yI(x,y) $$

They are used to calculate the centroid.

$$ \bar{x}=\frac{M_{10}}{M_{00}} $$
$$ \bar{y}=\frac{M_{01}}{M_{00}} $$

So:

  • Zeroth moment gives area.

  • First moments help calculate centroid.

22. Why Moments Are Useful?

Moments describe different characteristics of an object.

They are useful for:

  1. Measuring size.

  2. Finding object location.

  3. Finding orientation.

  4. Understanding shape.

  5. Analysing pixel distribution.

Higher-order moments capture more detailed structural information.

23. Hu Moments

Definition: Hu Moments are a set of seven moment-based shape descriptors.

They are designed to remain invariant to:

  1. Translation – moving an object.

  2. Scaling – increasing or decreasing its size.

  3. Rotation – turning an object.

geeksforgeeks.org

Example:

Imagine a triangle.

  • First, it is in its original position.

  • Then, it is rotated.

  • Then, its size is increased.

  • Then, it is moved.

Hu Moments provide a representation that remains relatively stable despite these transformations.

Applications: Object recognition and shape matching.

25. Texture Analysis

Definition: Texture analysis is the process of analysing the spatial arrangement and variation of intensity or colour in an image region.

Texture describes:

  • Smoothness.

  • Roughness.

  • Regularity.

  • Granularity.

  • Repetition.

  • Directionality.

Example:

A wall, wooden table and metal surface may have different textures.

26. Texture Examples

Smooth texture

Intensity changes very little.

Rough texture

Intensity changes frequently.

Regular texture

Repeating pattern.

Random texture

No fixed repeating pattern.

27. Why Texture Is Important?

Sometimes two objects have:

  • Similar colour.

  • Similar shape.

  • Similar size.

But their surface textures are different.

For example:

  • Wood has grain patterns.

  • Metal may have a smooth reflective surface.

Texture helps distinguish these objects.

Applications:

  1. Medical imaging.

  2. Remote sensing.

  3. Agriculture.

  4. Material classification.

  5. Industrial inspection.

  6. Face analysis.

  7. Surface defect detection.

28. Categories of Texture Analysis

There are three major categories.

1. Statistical methods

These analyse statistical properties of pixel values.

Examples:

  • Mean.

  • Variance.

  • Standard deviation.

  • Entropy.

  • GLCM features.

2. Structural methods

These consider texture as repeated geometric structures or basic patterns.

Example: A brick wall has repeated rectangular structures.

3. Transform-based methods

These analyse frequency information.

Examples:

  • Fourier Transform.

  • Wavelet Transform.

  • Gabor Filters.

29. First-Order Texture Features

First-order statistical features consider the distribution of individual pixel intensities.

They do not explicitly consider relationships between neighbouring pixels.

Examples:

  1. Mean.

  2. Variance.

  3. Standard deviation.

  4. Skewness.

  5. Kurtosis.

  6. Entropy.

Let's understand each.

30. Mean

Definition: Mean intensity is the average brightness of pixels in an image.

Formula:

$$ Mean=\mu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_i $$

Where:

  • N = Number of pixels.

  • $I_i$ = Pixel intensity.

Example:

Pixel values:

10, 20, 30, 40

$$ Mean=\frac{10+20+30+40}{4} $$
$$ =\frac{100}{4}=25 $$

Answer = 25.

31. Variance

Definition: Variance measures how much pixel intensities differ from their mean.

Formula:

$$ \sigma^2=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(I_i-\mu)^2 $$

Example:

Pixel values: 10, 20, 30

Mean:

$$ \mu=20 $$

Variance:

$$ =\frac{(10-20)^2+(20-20)^2+(30-20)^2}{3} $$
$$ =\frac{100+0+100}{3} $$
$$ \boxed{66.67} $$

Interpretation:

  • Low variance → Pixel values are similar.

  • High variance → Pixel values differ considerably.

32. Standard Deviation

Definition: Standard deviation measures the spread of pixel intensity values.

Formula:

$$ \sigma=\sqrt{Variance} $$

Using our previous example:

$$ \sigma=\sqrt{66.67} $$
$$ \boxed{\sigma\approx8.16} $$

Remember:

Variance is the square of standard deviation.

Standard deviation is expressed in the same units as the pixel intensity.

33. Entropy

Definition: Entropy measures the randomness or uncertainty of the intensity distribution.

Formula:

$$ H=-\sum_i p_i\log_2(p_i) $$

Where:

- $p_i$ = Probability of an intensity level.

Easy understanding:

  • Low entropy → Fewer intensity values dominate.

  • High entropy → More varied intensity values occur.

For example:

  • A completely uniform surface has low entropy.

  • A highly random, varied surface has higher entropy.

34. Simple Entropy Example

Suppose an image region contains two intensity values:

  • Intensity 0.

  • Intensity 1.

Both have equal probability:

$$ p_1=0.5,\quad p_2=0.5 $$

Formula:

$$ H=-\sum p_i\log_2(p_i) $$

Substitute:

$$ H=-[0.5\log_2(0.5)+0.5\log_2(0.5)] $$

We know:

$$ \log_2(0.5)=-1 $$

Therefore:

$$ H=-[-0.5-0.5] $$
$$ \boxed{H=1\ bit} $$

This is a very important numerical pattern.

35. Second-Order Texture Features

First-order statistics consider individual pixel values.

However, texture also depends on how neighbouring pixels are arranged.

For example:

Image A:

Plain text1111 1111 1111

Image B:

Plain text1010 0101 1010

Both contain similar numbers of 0s and 1s, but their arrangements are different.

Second-order features analyse these relationships.

The most common method is:

GLCM – Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix.

36. What Is GLCM?

Definition: GLCM describes how frequently pairs of gray levels occur at a specified distance and direction.

In simple words, it checks which pixel values occur next to which other pixel values.

It can examine:

  • Horizontal direction.

  • Vertical direction.

  • Diagonal direction.

  • Other diagonal direction.

It also considers the distance between pixels.

For example, we might check neighbouring pixels separated by one pixel horizontally.

37. Simple GLCM Concept

Suppose an image has only two gray levels:

  • 0 = Black.

  • 1 = White.

Consider neighbouring pixel pairs.

If the image contains many pairs of 0 followed by 0, then the corresponding GLCM entry becomes large.

Similarly, pairs of 0 followed by 1 increase another entry.

This allows GLCM to understand spatial relationships.

38. GLCM Matrix

For two gray levels, the GLCM can be represented as:

$$ GLCM= \begin{bmatrix} P(0,0)&P(0,1)\\ P(1,0)&P(1,1) \end{bmatrix} $$

Each element represents the frequency of a particular gray-level pair under a specified spatial relationship.

For example:

$$ GLCM= \begin{bmatrix} 4&2\\ 1&3 \end{bmatrix} $$

Meaning:

  • 4 times: 0 occurs next to 0.

  • 2 times: 0 occurs next to 1.

  • 1 time: 1 occurs next to 0.

  • 3 times: 1 occurs next to 1.

39. GLCM Normalization

Normalization converts the frequency values into probabilities.

Formula:

$$ P(i,j)=\frac{GLCM(i,j)}{\text{Total number of pairs}} $$

Using the previous matrix:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 4&2\\ 1&3 \end{bmatrix} $$

Total:

$$ 4+2+1+3=10 $$

Normalized GLCM:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 0.4&0.2\\ 0.1&0.3 \end{bmatrix} $$

Now the sum of all probabilities is:

$$ 0.4+0.2+0.1+0.3=1 $$

These probabilities are used to calculate GLCM features.

40. GLCM Contrast

Definition: Contrast measures local intensity variation between neighbouring pixels.

Formula:

$$ Contrast=\sum_{i,j}(i-j)^2P(i,j) $$

Using our normalized matrix:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 0.4&0.2\\ 0.1&0.3 \end{bmatrix} $$
$$ Contrast=0^2(0.4)+(-1)^2(0.2) $$
$$ +(1)^2(0.1)+0^2(0.3) $$
$$ =0+0.2+0.1+0 $$
$$ \boxed{Contrast=0.3} $$

Interpretation:

  • High contrast → Large intensity changes.

  • Low contrast → Similar neighbouring intensities.

41. GLCM Energy

Definition: Energy measures the uniformity of the GLCM distribution.

Formula:

$$ Energy=\sum_{i,j}P(i,j)^2 $$

Using our matrix:

$$ Energy=0.4^2+0.2^2+0.1^2+0.3^2 $$
$$ =0.16+0.04+0.01+0.09 $$
$$ \boxed{Energy=0.30} $$

Interpretation:

  • High energy → Distribution concentrated in fewer entries.

  • Low energy → Distribution more spread out.

42. GLCM Homogeneity

Definition: Homogeneity measures how close the GLCM values are to the diagonal.

Formula:

$$ Homogeneity=\sum_{i,j}\frac{P(i,j)}{1+(i-j)^2} $$

Using the same matrix:

$$ H=\frac{0.4}{1}+\frac{0.2}{2}+\frac{0.1}{2}+\frac{0.3}{1} $$
$$ =0.4+0.1+0.05+0.3 $$
$$ \boxed{H=0.85} $$

Interpretation:

  • High homogeneity → Neighbouring pixels tend to have similar intensities.

  • Low homogeneity → More differences between neighbouring pixels.

43. GLCM Correlation

Definition: Correlation measures the relationship between gray levels at neighbouring pixel positions.

It tells us whether neighbouring pixel values are related.

A common formula is:

$$ Correlation= \sum_{i,j} \frac{(i-\mu_i)(j-\mu_j)P(i,j)} {\sigma_i\sigma_j} $$

Where:

  • $\mu_i,\mu_j$ = Mean gray levels.

  • $\sigma_i,\sigma_j$ = Standard deviations.

Interpretation:

It measures the linear dependency between neighbouring pixel values.

Remember these five names and meanings for MCQs.

45. Texture Directionality

Definition: Texture directionality describes whether a texture has a preferred direction.

Example:

Plain text////////// ////////// //////////

The texture has a clear diagonal orientation.

GLCM can be calculated at different angles:

  • 0°

By comparing the results, the computer can identify texture directionality.

46. Texture Using Fourier Transform

Texture can also be analysed in the frequency domain.

Definition: Fourier Transform converts an image from the spatial domain into the frequency domain.

In simple words:

  • Spatial domain → How pixels are arranged in the image.

  • Frequency domain → How frequently intensity patterns change.

Formula:

$$ F(u,v)=\sum_x\sum_y f(x,y)e^{-j2\pi(ux/M+vy/N)} $$

Where:

  • $f(x,y)$ = Original image.

  • $F(u,v)$ = Frequency representation.

Periodic textures produce characteristic frequency components.

For example, regularly repeating stripes create recognizable frequency patterns.

47. Gabor Filters

Definition: Gabor filters are filters used to analyse texture by detecting patterns at particular orientations, frequencies and scales.

They are sensitive to:

  1. Orientation.

  2. Frequency.

  3. Scale.

Example:

A system can apply filters at:

  • 0°.

  • 45°.

  • 90°.

  • 135°.

It then compares the responses to identify the texture.

mdpi.com

Applications:

  • Texture classification.

  • Face analysis.

  • Surface inspection.

  • Pattern recognition.

48. Wavelet-Based Texture Analysis

Definition: Wavelet Transform analyses an image at multiple scales.

In simple words, it allows the computer to study both large and small texture patterns.

It decomposes the image into components representing different:

  • Frequencies.

  • Scales.

  • Orientations.

Applications:

  1. Texture classification.

4. Bounding Box

Definition: A bounding box is a rectangle drawn around an object to show its location in an image.

There are two common representations.

4.1 Corner Representation

It uses four coordinates:

$$ (x_{\min},y_{\min},x_{\max},y_{\max}) $$

Where:

  • $x_{\min}$ = Left coordinate.

  • $y_{\min}$ = Top coordinate.

  • $x_{\max}$ = Right coordinate.

  • $y_{\max}$ = Bottom coordinate.

Example:

$$ x_{\min}=50 $$
$$ y_{\min}=100 $$
$$ x_{\max}=250 $$
$$ y_{\max}=400 $$

Width:

$$ 250-50=200 $$

Height:

$$ 400-100=300 $$

4.2 Center-Width-Height Representation

Instead of using the corners, we represent the box using:

  • $x_c$ = Centre X-coordinate.

  • $y_c$ = Centre Y-coordinate.

  • $w$ = Width.

  • $h$ = Height.

The representation is:

$$ (x_c,y_c,w,h) $$

Conversion from corner representation:

$$ x_c=\frac{x_{\min}+x_{\max}}{2} $$
$$ y_c=\frac{y_{\min}+y_{\max}}{2} $$
$$ w=x_{\max}-x_{\min} $$
$$ h=y_{\max}-y_{\min} $$

Numerical example:

Given:

$$ (50,100,250,400) $$

Centre X:

$$ x_c=\frac{50+250}{2}=150 $$

Centre Y:

$$ y_c=\frac{100+400}{2}=250 $$

Width:

$$ w=250-50=200 $$

Height:

$$ h=400-100=300 $$

Final answer:

$$ \boxed{(150,250,200,300)} $$

11. IoU – Intersection over Union

Very important numerical

Definition: IoU measures how much two bounding boxes overlap.

The two boxes are:

  1. Predicted bounding box – The box generated by the model.

  2. Ground-truth bounding box – The actual correct box.

Formula:

$$ \boxed{IoU=\frac{Intersection\ Area}{Union\ Area}} $$

Where:

  • Intersection = Common overlapping area.

  • Union = Total area covered by both boxes.

Union can be calculated as:

$$ Union=A_1+A_2-Intersection $$

Numerical example from your notes:

Given:

  • Predicted box area = 100.

  • Ground-truth box area = 120.

  • Intersection = 80.

Step 1: Calculate union.

$$ Union=100+120-80 $$
$$ Union=140 $$

Step 2: Calculate IoU.

$$ IoU=\frac{80}{140} $$
$$ \boxed{IoU=0.5714} $$

Therefore, IoU is approximately 0.57 or 57.14%.

Remember: Higher IoU means better overlap between predicted and actual bounding boxes.

12. Why IoU Is Important?

Suppose the actual object is surrounded by a ground-truth box.

The detector produces a predicted box.

If the predicted box overlaps significantly with the ground-truth box, IoU will be high.

If the boxes overlap very little, IoU will be low.

Therefore:

  • High IoU → Good localization.

  • Low IoU → Poor localization.

IoU is commonly used to decide whether a predicted bounding box sufficiently matches the ground truth.

13. Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS)

Very important exam topic

Definition: Non-Maximum Suppression is a technique used to remove redundant or overlapping bounding boxes around the same object.

Why is NMS needed?

Suppose an object detector identifies one car but produces three boxes:

Plain textCar Box 1 = 0.95 Car Box 2 = 0.89 Car Box 3 = 0.73

All three boxes may represent the same car.

We don't need three boxes around one car.

We need the best one.

NMS removes the unnecessary boxes.

naknaklee.github.io

NMS procedure:

  1. Select the box with the highest confidence.

  2. Calculate IoU with the other boxes.

  3. Remove boxes whose IoU exceeds the chosen threshold.

  4. Select the next highest-confidence remaining box.

  5. Repeat.

Example:

Before NMS:

Plain textCar → 0.95 Car → 0.89 Car → 0.73

After NMS:

Important: NMS removes duplicate detections. It should not remove genuinely separate objects simply because they are nearby.

29. Euclidean Distance

Definition: Euclidean distance measures the straight-line distance between two points.

For two feature vectors:

$$ A=(x_1,y_1) $$
$$ B=(x_2,y_2) $$

Formula:

$$ \boxed{d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}} $$

For n-dimensional data:

$$ d(A,B)=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(A_i-B_i)^2} $$

Numerical example:

Find the distance between:

$$ A=(2,3),\quad B=(5,7) $$

Step 1:

$$ d=\sqrt{(5-2)^2+(7-3)^2} $$

Step 2:

$$ =\sqrt{3^2+4^2} $$

Step 3:

$$ =\sqrt{9+16} $$
$$ =\sqrt{25} $$
$$ \boxed{d=5} $$

30. K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)

Definition: KNN is a classification algorithm that predicts the class of an unknown object based on the majority class among its K nearest training samples.

Difference between NN and KNN:

  • NN considers only one nearest point.

  • KNN considers K nearest points.

Example:

Suppose K = 5.

Among the five nearest training samples:

  • Class A = 4.

  • Class B = 1.

The majority class is A.

Therefore:

$$ \boxed{Prediction=Class\ A} $$

Steps of KNN:

  1. Choose K.

  2. Calculate distances.

  3. Find K nearest neighbours.

  4. Count the classes.

  5. Select the majority class.

32. Importance of Feature Scaling

This is a very important concept in KNN.

Suppose our features are:

  • Age = 20–60.

  • Income = 20,000–200,000.

Income has much larger numerical values than age.

When calculating Euclidean distance, income may dominate the calculation.

This can lead to incorrect classifications.

Solution: Normalization

Min-Max normalization converts values into a common range, generally 0 to 1.

Formula:

$$ \boxed{x'=\frac{x-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}} $$

Example:

Minimum income = 20,000.

Maximum income = 200,000.

Given income = 110,000.

$$ x'=\frac{110000-20000}{200000-20000} $$
$$ =\frac{90000}{180000} $$
$$ \boxed{x'=0.5} $$

Now the value is between 0 and 1.

40. Performance Measures for Object Detection

Two fundamental measures are:

  1. Precision.

  2. Recall.

Before understanding them, know these three terms.

True Positive (TP): An object is correctly detected.

False Positive (FP): The detector predicts an object, but that prediction is incorrect.

False Negative (FN): An actual object is missed by the detector.

Precision formula:

$$ \boxed{Precision=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}} $$

Precision answers:

"Out of all the objects predicted as positive, how many were actually correct?"

Recall formula:

$$ \boxed{Recall=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}} $$

Recall answers:

"Out of all actual objects, how many did the detector find?"

41. Example of Precision and Recall

Suppose a detector identifies 10 objects.

Among them:

  • 8 are correct.

  • 2 are incorrect.

Therefore:

$$ TP=8,\quad FP=2 $$

Step 1: Precision

$$ Precision=\frac{8}{8+2} $$
$$ =\frac{8}{10}=0.8 $$
$$ \boxed{Precision=80\%} $$

Step 2: Recall

Suppose there were actually 10 objects in the image and the detector found 8.

Therefore:

$$ TP=8,\quad FN=2 $$
$$ Recall=\frac{8}{8+2} $$
$$ =\frac{8}{10}=0.8 $$
$$ \boxed{Recall=80\%} $$

Remember the difference:

  • Precision = How many predictions were correct?

  • Recall = How many actual objects were found?
